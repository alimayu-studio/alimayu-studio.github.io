Why congregations

The most trusted room a family walks into

Many families, especially Black and brown families, face real barriers to care, from long waits to being dismissed by providers. Public health often plans by zip code. But families describe themselves by their faith, their church, and their people. Clergy are present for the grandmother, the grandchild, and the great-grandchild. Congregational Home Visiting starts there.

How it works

From blessing to a healthier childhood

ABIDE with FAAITH builds on Congregational Relationships for Early-childhood Equity and Development (CREED), the model Dr. Alimayu and Rev. Dr. Armstrong created. It weaves early childhood science into rituals that faith communities have practiced for centuries.

  1. PrepareBefore the blessing, families take part in short sessions on early brain development, safe sleep, and positive parenting.
  2. VisitClergy visit families at home, bringing trust that outside programs have to earn.
  3. BlessClergy perform the baby blessing in front of the whole faith community, which surrounds the family with support.
  4. AdvocateCongregations use what families share to push for fair access to care, services, and resources.

The story

A pastor, a researcher, and a question

Rev. Dr. Darrell LaRue Armstrong has pastored Shiloh Baptist Church, Trenton’s first Black Baptist church, since 2000. A former assistant commissioner over New Jersey’s Division of Child Abuse Prevention and board president of the Association of Professionals Solving the Abuse of Children (APSAC), he saw each baby blessing as a missed chance to share what science knows about a child’s first years.

Dr. Alimayu, then directing networks and policy at the HOPE National Resource Center at Tufts Medical Center, partnered with him to formalize that work into a model any faith tradition could use. With funding from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, they tested it together as co-investigators.

Questions people ask

What is Congregational Home Visiting?

Congregational Home Visiting is an initiative of Alimayu Studio and FAAITH. Clergy visit families at home to support early relational health: the safe, stable, and nurturing relationships that help young children thrive. It builds on the CREED model, which Dr. Alimayu and Rev. Dr. Armstrong created.

What is FAAITH and HOPE?

FAAITH and HOPE is the project that developed and studied the CREED model. It was a partnership between FAAITH (Faith-leaders Allied and Aligned to Institute Trust in the Home) and the HOPE National Resource Center at Tufts Medical Center, studied with funding from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation’s Systems for Action program at Shiloh Baptist Church in Trenton, New Jersey.

What is CREED, and how does it relate to ABIDE with FAAITH?

Congregational Relationships for Early-childhood Equity and Development (CREED) joins child-centered religious rituals, such as baby blessings, dedications, and christenings, with research on early childhood development. Dr. Alimayu and Rev. Dr. Armstrong created CREED and studied it as FAAITH and HOPE. That research provided the foundation for ABIDE with FAAITH, a new effort built on what CREED showed. Details on the CREED research are available through Systems for Action.

Who leads Congregational Home Visiting?

Dr. Allison Alimayu (published as Allison Stephens) and Rev. Dr. Darrell Armstrong, pastor of Shiloh Baptist Church and founder of FAAITH. The two co-led the FAAITH and HOPE research as co-investigators.

Is there training for faith leaders?

Yes. Congregational Home Visiting is building on-demand training for faith leaders. Those who complete it earn a verifiable digital micro-credential. Join the list on this page to hear first.

Can my congregation or organization take part?

Yes. Faith leaders, seminaries, health systems, and funders can request clergy training, the parent curriculum, a keynote, or a partnership through the form on this page.
