Why congregations

The most trusted room a family walks into

Many families, especially Black and brown families, face real barriers to care, from long waits to being dismissed by providers. Public health often plans by zip code. But families describe themselves by their faith, their church, and their people. Clergy are present for the grandmother, the grandchild, and the great-grandchild. FAAITH and HOPE starts there.

The CREED model

From blessing to a healthier childhood

Congregational Relationships for Early-childhood Equity and Development (CREED) weaves early childhood science into rituals that faith communities have practiced for centuries.

  1. PrepareBefore the blessing, families take part in short sessions on early brain development, safe sleep, and positive parenting.
  2. VisitTrained members of the congregation visit families at home, bringing trust that outside programs have to earn.
  3. BlessClergy perform the baby blessing in front of the whole faith community, which surrounds the family with support.
  4. AdvocateCongregations use what families share to push for fair access to care, services, and resources.

What is inside

Five parts of the model

Built with clergy and community members, and informed by Dr. Bruce Perry of the Child Trauma Academy and Dr. Charlyn Harper Browne of the Center for the Study of Social Policy.

i.

Clergy training

A curriculum and guidebook that equips faith leaders to teach early childhood development.

ii.

Parent leadership

A workbook-based series that helps parents build positive childhood experiences and advocate for their children.

iii.

Faith and science

The HOPE framework from Tufts Medical Center, joined with scripture and the Protective Factors Framework.

iv.

REAL Impact survey

A racial equity assessment that shows how systemic racism plays out when families seek care.

v.

Systems change

Guidance for congregations to work with policymakers on the barriers families name.

The story

A pastor, a researcher, and a question

Rev. Dr. Darrell Armstrong blesses ten to twelve babies a year at Shiloh Baptist Church, Trenton’s first Black Baptist church. As a former director of New Jersey’s child abuse prevention division, he saw each blessing as a missed chance to share what science knows about a baby’s first years.

Dr. Alimayu, then directing networks and policy at the HOPE National Resource Center at Tufts Medical Center, partnered with him to formalize that work into a model any faith tradition could use. With funding from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, they tested it together as co-investigators.

Questions people ask

What is FAAITH and HOPE?

FAAITH (Faith-leaders Allied and Aligned to Institute Trust in the Home) and HOPE (Healthy Outcomes from Positive Experiences) is a faith community and academic partnership that brings the science of positive childhood experiences into congregations. It was studied with funding from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation’s Systems for Action program at Shiloh Baptist Church in Trenton, New Jersey.

What is the CREED model?

Congregational Relationships for Early-childhood Equity and Development (CREED) joins child-centered religious rituals, such as baby blessings, dedications, and christenings, with research on early childhood development. It includes clergy training, a parent leadership and advocacy curriculum, a racial equity assessment, and guidance for systems change.

Who leads FAAITH and HOPE?

Dr. Allison Alimayu (published as Allison Stephens) co-led the research as co-investigator while at the HOPE National Resource Center at Tufts Medical Center, in partnership with Rev. Dr. Darrell Armstrong, pastor of Shiloh Baptist Church and founder of FAAITH.

Can my congregation or organization use the CREED model?

Yes. Faith leaders, seminaries, health systems, and funders can request clergy training, the parent curriculum, a keynote, or a partnership through the form on this page.
