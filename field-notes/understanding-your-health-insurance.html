Understanding Your Health Insurance: The Terms, What Is Covered, and What Is Not

Health plans use words that confuse almost everyone. This guide explains the key terms, what most plans must cover, and how to push back when a claim is denied.

By Allison Alimayu, PhD · November 17, 2026 · 3 minute read

Most people learn how their health plan works when a bill arrives. Knowing a few terms ahead of time can save money and stress.

The Words on Every Plan

  • Premium: the amount paid each month to keep the plan, even in a month with no visits.
  • Deductible: the amount paid each year before the plan starts paying for most services.
  • Copay: a set fee for a service, such as $30 for an office visit.
  • Coinsurance: a share of the bill paid after the deductible, such as 20 percent.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum: the most a member pays in one year for covered, in-network care. After that, the plan pays the full cost.
  • In-network: doctors, labs, and hospitals that have a contract with the plan. Out-of-network care usually costs more or is not covered.
  • Prior authorization: approval the plan requires before some tests, drugs, or procedures.

Where to Find What Is Covered

Every plan must provide a Summary of Benefits and Coverage. This short document lists the costs of common services side by side. Employers and plans must share it, and most plans post it on the member website.

After each claim, the plan sends an Explanation of Benefits. It is not a bill. It shows what the provider charged, what the plan paid, and what may still be owed. Compare it with any bill before paying.

What Most Plans Must Cover

Under the Affordable Care Act, most plans must cover many preventive services at no cost when the provider is in network. These include many screenings, vaccines, well-child visits, and prenatal visits. Most plans must also cover breastfeeding support and breast pumps.

Watch for one catch. A preventive visit can turn into a billed visit when a new problem comes up. Ask at the start how the visit will be billed.

What Is Often Not Covered

  • Out-of-network care, except in emergencies.
  • Services the plan decides are not medically necessary.
  • Drugs that are not on the plan's drug list, called the formulary.
  • Most dental and vision care, which often needs separate coverage.

When the Plan Says No

  • Ask for the denial in writing, with the reason and the plan rule behind it.
  • File an internal appeal. For most plans, the deadline is 180 days from the denial notice.
  • If the plan still says no, request an external review by an independent reviewer. For most plans, the request must be made within four months.
  • Keep a log of every call: the date, the name of the person, and what was said.

Since 2022, the No Surprises Act has protected most people from surprise bills for emergency care and for some out-of-network care at in-network hospitals. A surprise bill is worth questioning before it is paid.
